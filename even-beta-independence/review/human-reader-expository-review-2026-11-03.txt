Manuscript: Linear independence of even Dirichlet beta values
Revision: the current 17-page local manuscript, with the h=1 example.
PDF SHA256: d9c2f5ab11255e9ff2f7f9719ff36c71699b101f975d93fd495bd159fd2dce1b

The intended reader for this review knows linear algebra, power series,
complex analysis, and basic linear differential systems, but has not
studied Fischler's construction. The three imported propositions are
available as stated inputs. The question is whether the manuscript
helps that reader acquire the organizing ideas of the proof.

The main remaining weakness is that the reasons for several constructions
are distributed across distant sections. The text usually defines the
objects precisely and explains how to manipulate them. The reader still
has to assemble why these are the right objects and how their different
roles fit together. A parameter table helps recall notation, but cannot
by itself supply that causal explanation.

The next revision should concentrate on the six connections below. The
first four have the greatest effect on a first reading. They call for
short explanations and some reordering within passages, while preserving
the mathematical statements and the overall organization.

1. Explain why the construction needs a growing number of families

Locations: paper/sections/introduction.tex:142; forms.tex:11 and 30;
rank.tex:19; conclusion.tex:43. Section filenames after the first are
relative to paper/sections/ throughout this report.

The introduction uses h proportional to a before introducing h. More
substantively, Section 3 tells the reader that differentiating F_n in t
produces several families and raises the possible beta arguments, but
leaves the need for those extra families to be reconstructed from the
rank proof. The reader encounters a cost before understanding its benefit.

Introduce h as the largest t-derivative order before its first use, and
give the reason for using F_n through F_n^(h): the rank argument needs
enough independent scalar remainders to force a contradiction. Each of
the h+1 families contributes one of these remainders at each preimage of
the evaluation point. With r, kappa and omega fixed, the rank inequality

  (h+1)(kappa-2r) + omega > a

requires h to grow proportionally to a. The resulting beta cutoff is at
most a+h. State this tradeoff informally early, then connect it to the
displayed inequality where the rank condition is introduced.

The existing parameter table can remain a compact reference. Its role
should be to reinforce an explanation the reader has already received.

2. Distinguish the purposes of omega and Omega

Locations: inputs.tex:51 and 88; estimates.tex:58; rank.tex:340.

The manuscript distinguishes the two thresholds formally, but a reader
may still treat both as parameters measuring the same kind of decay.
Their different jobs are central to the construction's efficiency.

Explain the rational-function input in three parts before its full
formulas: bounded integer partial-fraction coefficients, exact vanishing
of the first Laurent coefficients, and additional smallness of the next
coefficients. Then assign a purpose to each threshold:

  - omega controls the guaranteed order of vanishing. It leaves enough
    decay after z-differentiation, requiring kappa < omega, and supplies
    the zeros used above z=1 in the rank count.
  - Omega controls additional exponential smallness. The stronger
    Laurent-coefficient estimate supplies the factor r^(-Omega*n) in
    the tail bound, even though the guaranteed vanishing order remains
    omega*n.

This explains why omega can remain fixed while Omega grows like
sqrt(a log a). It also makes the two factors in (5.4),
r^(-Omega*n) and (M/x)^(omega*n), readable as separate contributions.
The later parameter balance will then have an established meaning.

3. Explain the differential rows as one mechanism with two uses

Locations: forms.tex:67 and 130; rank.tex:116; conclusion.tex:15.

The product-rule recurrence is correct and the universal identity is
stated. However, a human reader must recognize for themselves that this
single mechanism is what connects the small numerical forms to the
kernel contradiction several pages later.

Before the component recurrence, suppress the family index temporarily,
write the system as y'=A y, and display the organizing identity

  (C y)' = (C' + C A)y.

The derivative can therefore be represented by a new coefficient row
without changing the vector of functions. Iterating gives the Q rows.
These rows depend on the differential system and the initial polynomial
row, and work for every solution of that system.

Make the two uses explicit at this point. For the beta-valued solution,
the rows represent derivatives that become the small integer forms after
factorial normalization and denominator clearing. For the solution with
a hypothetical rational kernel vector as its initial value, the same
rows express derivatives that vanish. This explains why introducing the
arbitrary solution g in Section 6 is useful.

Also explain why the odd-indexed functions remain in the system after
their values at -1 have vanished. Their derivatives need not vanish:
the displayed system gives H_1(-1)=0 but H_1'(-1)=-1. Keep the full system
while differentiating, then use the zero odd initial values when forming
the beta linear forms. This one example prevents an avoidable question
about why the proof carries apparently unnecessary coordinates.

4. Show the integration mechanism before the denominator machinery

Locations: arithmetic.tex:12, 55 and 89.

The text already states that integration denominators are distinct and
explains that harmonic growth is better than linear growth. The missing
piece is a concrete route by which the reader can discover that fact.
At present the distinct-product LCM and its valuation proof arrive
before the reader has seen an integration produce such a product.

Give a two-integration example before defining Delta. Starting with a
monomial of degree m1, integrate, multiply by a monomial of degree m2,
and integrate again. For nonnegative m1 and m2, the resulting term is

  u^(m1+m2+2) / [(m1+1)(m1+m2+2)].

The two denominator factors are successive integration degrees, so the
second is strictly larger. The same mechanism produces an increasing
list of degrees in every term of an iterated integral. This motivates
the definition of Delta; the formal proof then handles all depths and
the additional powers of 4.

Connect the saving to the theorem's scale explicitly. When d is of order
a and k is of order n, a naive cost proportional to d*k would contribute
an exponent of order a*n, overwhelming the intended decay on the
sqrt(a log a)*n scale. The distinct-product estimate reduces this cost
to order n log a. This is a decisive part of the argument.

5. Complete two conceptual transitions in the rank proof

Locations: rank.tex:133, 218, 257 and 297.

The h=1 example now explains the common polynomial row well. Preserve it.
One further sentence can explain the choice of logarithms before the
example: L'=-1/z matches the coefficient that links successive components
of the differential system. Powers of L are therefore suited to building
the additional chain of components. The subsequent binomial identities
verify that design.

At the transition to the residue argument, distinguish independent
solution vectors from independent scalar remainders. A fixed row can
annihilate a nonzero vector, so independence of the Y_q alone would not
justify counting all h+1 remainders. The residue lemma rules out the
relevant rational-function relation between components of any nonzero
combination. The nonzero highest polynomial P_b then prevents its scalar
remainder from being identically zero. State this reason before the
descent calculation, so the reader knows what that calculation must do.

A short reminder can also connect the two diagonal cancellations. The
local integrating factor in Section 4 controls denominators. The
quadratic substitution in Section 6 performs the analogous cancellation
in a form that keeps the common coefficient row polynomial and permits
the residue argument. The algebra has related origins but distinct jobs.

6. Present the numerical choices as a sequence of constraints

Locations: conclusion.tex:34 and 43; estimates.tex:21.

The conclusion repeats much of the introduction's square-root explanation
and then imports the numerical values. Preserve the attribution to
Fischler, but use this space to explain how the choices fit together:

  First choose fixed r, kappa and omega with 2r < kappa < omega. Then
  choose the proportion h/a to satisfy the rank requirement. Finally
  choose Omega on the sqrt(a log a) scale, balancing its quadratic
  coefficient cost against the logarithmic denominator cost.

For the actual values, the rank slope is particularly instructive:

  (9/25)(529/50 - 2*39/10) = 1251/1250 > 1.

That calculation makes the small strict margin visible. The reader can
then follow the separate contributions to the final quotient: the
dominant decay is Omega log r, the coefficient cost contains
Omega^2 log r/(2a), and the denominator contribution is kappa log a.
Explain these terms alongside the existing asymptotics, without adding
an optimization argument or claiming the choices are optimal.

One small clarification

At forms.tex:164 the reader may wonder why k starts at 2M+2 when the
finite correction has degree at most 2M-1. The chosen derivative order
k-1 is one higher than the minimum needed to remove that correction.
Say that the later estimates and Taylor correction use this convenient
range. This is an explanation of the existing choice, not a request to
change the bound.

What should be preserved

The opening explanation of the dimension bound and its distinction from
irrationality of a specified beta value is clear. The interpretation of
the weak rank condition as a statement about the first column is useful.
The h=1 example exposes the fixed-row mechanism. The six-point overview
now explains the origin of the orders and the role of the infinity
family in reducing the degree factor. The separation between the limits
n -> infinity and a -> infinity is stated explicitly. These passages
provide good models for the remaining revisions.

The recommended pass should let a reader explain why the additional
families are needed, why omega and Omega have separate roles, why one
differential-row calculation serves both size and rank, and why the
denominator saving is large enough. It should also let them say why
independence of the scalar remainders requires a separate argument.
Adding detail to every calculation would obscure those aims.
