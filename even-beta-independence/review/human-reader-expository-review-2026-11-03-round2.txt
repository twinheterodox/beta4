The intended reader knows linear algebra, power series, complex analysis
and basic linear differential systems, but has not studied Fischler's
construction. The three imported propositions are treated as stated
inputs. This is an exposition review of the full manuscript, with
particular attention to the explanation of the square-root scale.

Overall judgment

The previous revision substantially improves the route through the
argument. The reasons for the additional families, the different roles
of omega and Omega, the two uses of the differential rows, and the
denominator saving are now explicit. The earlier review's seven findings
have been addressed. The square-root scale is explained both in the
introduction and in the final parameter choice.

The remaining recommendations are narrower: two local bridges in the
rank proof, followed by an optional improvement to the visibility of the
square-root explanation. I do not recommend another broad expansion of
the paper. The question in each case is what a reader still has to
reconstruct, not whether the displayed identities can be checked.

R1. Show the differential cancellation in the h=1 example
Priority: medium; the most useful remaining change.
Locations: paper/sections/rank.tex:148, 171 and 210.

The example now successfully explains how different families can use
one fixed polynomial row. However, its derivative check verifies only
the first two components, (0,1) and (1,L). The unfamiliar and decisive
components are L*g_j - j*g_(j+1). The text asserts that these satisfy the
same enlarged system, then postpones the explanation to the general
binomial calculation.

The reader therefore understands where the family index has gone before
understanding why the new functions still form a common differential
system. The needed cancellation is small enough to show in the example.
Set

  Z_j = L*g_j - j*g_(j+1).

For j >= 2, the base system and L'=-1/z give

  Z_j' = -g_j/z + L*(-g_(j-1)/z - g_j/(2z))
                    - j*(-g_j/z - g_(j+1)/(2z))
       = -Z_(j-1)/z - Z_j/(2z).

At the first link, Z_1' = 2L/[z(1-z)] - Z_1/(2z). These are precisely
the required lower equations for the h=1 column. The calculation makes
the coefficient j understandable: differentiation changes it to j-1,
which supplies the previous component.

Replace the easy first-two-component check with this calculation, or
add the essential line and shorten the surrounding explanation. Keep
the general formulas and their verification. This is an illustration
of the existing argument, not a correction to its identities.

R2. Expose the coefficient correspondence in the Laplace-transform step
Priority: medium.
Location: paper/sections/rank.tex:356, especially lines 365-374.

The six-point overview tells the reader that Laurent vanishing at
infinity supplies zeros above z=1. The proof eventually gives the
Laplace-transform identity, but passes directly from it to the assertion
that the coefficient of t^(-k-1) is G^(k)(0). This is the crucial bridge
between two different locations and two different functions.

Make the correspondence visible with the elementary identity

  integral_0^infinity exp(-t*u) * u^k/k! du = t^(-k-1)
  (t > 0, k a nonnegative integer).

Then identify A_(k+1)=G^(k)(0) in the large-t expansion. The zeros of
A_1,...,A_(omega*n-1) become the zeros of the first omega*n-1 Taylor
coefficients of G at zero. Finally G(u)=R_n(exp(-u)) transfers that
order to R_n at z=1, since exp(-u)=1-u+O(u^2).

The manuscript already contains the correct correspondence and the
change of variable. This recommendation displays why they work. State
the correspondence as an asymptotic expansion, or derive it from the
finite partial-fraction expression; do not justify it by unrestricted
termwise integration of an arbitrary local Taylor series over the
whole positive real axis. Two or three explanatory lines should suffice.

R3. Make the square-root balance easier to locate at a glance
Priority: low; presentation refinement, not a missing explanation.
Locations: paper/sections/introduction.tex:148 and
paper/sections/conclusion.tex:42.

The current prose does flag where the square root comes from. It names
the linear decay gain in Omega, the quadratic coefficient cost Omega^2/a,
and the logarithmic denominator cost. It also explains the conversion
from a to the beta cutoff s. No new mechanism needs to be supplied.

For greater visibility, replace part of the prose in the introduction
with a short displayed chain, explicitly described as a scale comparison:

  Omega^2/a of order log a
       => Omega of order sqrt(a log a),

  dimension-bound scale = sqrt(a log a)/log a = sqrt(a/log a).

Lead it with a direct sentence such as: "The square-root scale comes
from the quadratic cost of increasing Omega." Explain that increasing
Omega to this scale gains decay while keeping coefficient growth on
the logarithmic scale. This makes the choice easy to recall without
introducing an optimization claim or repeating the final asymptotic
calculation. The existing explanation is already adequate if brevity
is preferred; this item is optional.

What now works well

The opening interpretation of the dimension bound makes the result's
meaning clear. The weak rank condition is explained in elementary
column language. Section 3 distinguishes the two derivative indices
and explains why odd functions remain during differentiation. The
matrix product rule connects the numerical forms with the kernel
argument. Section 4 now presents an example before its denominator
machinery and explains why the saving matters at the theorem's scale.
The six-point overview gives the rank proof a visible target, including
the special role of the infinity family. The conclusion presents the
parameter constraints before the numerical values and displays the
strict margin in the rank condition.

These passages should be preserved. Routine index changes, the LCM
valuation proof, and the norm estimates do not need expansion for the
stated audience. The principal opportunities left are the two short
rank-proof connections above.
